Illicit Tobacco Trade

Economic Drivers, Organised Crime and European Security

A discussion on the changing nature of illicit tobacco trade, its economic drivers and the evolution of criminal networks, with particular attention to the impact on legal markets, public revenues and European security.

Why this discussion matters

Illicit tobacco trade presents an evolving economic, fiscal and law-enforcement challenge.

Alongside traditional cross-border smuggling, criminal networks operate through increasingly complex supply chains, encompassing the sourcing of materials, illicit production, logistics, counterfeiting, direct distribution to consumers and the use of digital channels.

Held on the initiative of Member of the European Parliament Flavio Tosi and organised by DYNAMES – Fondazione Boscacci ETS, the discussion will examine the interplay between economic incentives, organised crime, enforcement capacity, corruption, geography, affordability and technology.

The aim is to move beyond a purely fiscal interpretation, exploring why illicit markets become established, how criminal organisations adapt and what these developments mean for European security.

The analytical foundation: Oxford Economics

The discussion will draw on the September 2026 Oxford Economics report, An Economic Assessment of the Drivers of Illicit Trade, combining a literature review with an analysis of nine markets: Australia, Bangladesh, Brazil, Chile, France, Malaysia, the Netherlands, Pakistan and Ukraine.

According to the report, illicit trade increased over the decade reviewed in eight of the nine countries studied; tax revenues foregone as a result of illicit trade also rose in eight of those markets.

The report identifies organised crime, enforcement effectiveness, corruption, geography, product affordability, cultural norms and technology as key drivers shaping the evolution of illicit markets.

An evolving criminal business model

Criminal organisations are increasingly relocating production closer to consumer markets, using online platforms, social media, encrypted messaging and cross-border delivery networks.

This transformation broadens the challenge: action at the border alone is no longer enough; the entire criminal supply chain, from production to distribution, must be considered.

Why it matters for Europe

France and the Netherlands provide two useful cases for understanding the changing phenomenon: the growth of counterfeiting, the shift of production closer to end markets, online distribution and the presence of illegal production facilities within Europe.

A note on the data. For France and the Netherlands, the report uses data on duty-not-paid products, a broader category than illicit trade alone: in some cases, it also includes lawful cross-border purchases not subject to domestic excise duties. This distinction is essential when interpreting market shares.

Key takeaways

The discussion will focus on five insights highlighted in the analytical report:

  • Illicit trade results from the interaction of economic incentives with criminal, institutional and market factors.
  • Production may become more local, while criminal activity retains a transnational dimension.
  • Digital distribution expands the reach and reduces the visibility of illicit channels, calling for new investigative capabilities.
  • Alongside enforcement, prevention and early disruption of supply chains are essential: entrenched criminal networks are harder and more costly to dismantle.
  • European cooperation and the timely sharing of information are central to tackling adaptable criminal networks.

Methodological note

The main analytical reference is the Oxford Economics report, An Economic Assessment of the Drivers of Illicit Trade (September 2026). The data and conclusions attributed to the report should be interpreted in light of its methodology, definitions and underlying sources. The report also specifies that its literature review excluded studies carried out or funded by the tobacco industry.
